Chapter 1

The idea: a resort island where the budget lives

Wii Sports Resort works because Wuhu Island is one legible place. Every activity has a home you can point to from the air, the flyover is the tour, and nothing on it is a menu. The Horizon does the same with Hearth's seven buildings, in Hearth's paper-card style, with the Journey map as the spine.

The rule that shapes everything: you should be able to see where you're going. From the harbour square you see the dam and the Crown. From the Crown you see the whole island and the lighthouse at the far edge. From the plane you see all of it and understand it in one loop. That is why the island is a hook around a lagoon, why the mountain is off-centre, and why the beach is long and straight: sightlines and flight lines were drawn before any building was placed.

The second rule: movement is the content. The three networks from the pitch grow to ten ways to move, each with its own geometry, camera and way of stopping. Skate lines breathe (fast in the open, slow through neighbourhoods); gliders launch from heights the land provides; the plane has a real strip and a real dock; the mountain has four doors. The money tools stay exactly where they are and one tap away.

The third rule is Hearth's own: play earns nothing. No pastime scores, unlocks or nudges a budget. The reward for finding a lantern is that it stays lit.

Chapter 1½

Two scales of one place

The Journey map is not a menu leading to the app; it is the app at its most useful scale. The world is that same experience when you step inside. One island, one household model, two renderers.

The map is home

Sign-in lands on the Journey map: the Horizon drawn low-poly from the same coordinates, framed from the south-east so Little Harbour is nearest and the Crown stands behind (page A, seen from above). The current month’s camp is lit wherever it is; the camp card is docked at the bottom with Everyday · now, Prepare/Protect/Build, what is leaving and when, what changed since last time, Hercules’s corner. Every ordinary task is done here: record a purchase, move a bill on the timeline strip, open a book, talk to Hercules. Nothing ordinary requires stepping in.

Stepping in is a change of scale

Both renderers draw the same WorldDefinition. The Journey map is the island at LOD 0 (≤ 40 k triangles, the whole island); the detailed world is LOD 2–3 with streaming. One orbit camera runs through Sky, Region, Stop and Up close, then hands to the world’s Look and Walk cameras. What you tapped at Region is what you stand beside at Up close. The cloud crossfade that shipped this week stays as the fallback for reduced motion, no WebGL, or a slow load.

Time walks the island

The Year Walk is a trail through twelve stations, one per calendar month, placed where the island’s season is strongest: January on the snowy Shoulder, June on Long Sands with the lupines, October in the red maples of the north pass, December in Little Harbour under Lantern Row. Each station is a garden of beds, one bed per month-year; a bed holds the month’s summary in planting cards (the same eighteen scores and recipes the Journey uses today, translated from land to beds). Appending history never moves a bed. Era gates stand where eras ended. The walk ahead is stakes and string. Households become distinct in their beds, gates, sculptures and found lanterns, on an island that is the same for everyone.

Everyday life lives at the homestead

The land around Our home is the household’s own: the kitchen garden that matures with qualifying food weeks, the landing where income arrives as cargo, the workbench, a place in the making that becomes the ready pavilion at 100 % backed (fully backed is not bought or experienced), and the timber crossing over the town channel. That crossing is the one place physical wear ever shows: planks go only after three consecutive confirmed strain days, come back on the same site after two clear days, plants first, then timber, then stone; incomplete evidence freezes both. The house itself never wears. The island never wears.

One model

Household state and history → one WorldOverlay, derived on read, pure, never stored → the Journey renderer, the world renderer and the Desk. Editing, deleting, refunding or reclassifying re-derives; “what changed here” on any bed or site is a read of the receipts.

Four tiers of interaction

  • Glance: the camp card and explicit labels on the map
  • On-map: drag a bill on the strip, tap a bed, tap Hercules
  • Compact panel: tap a building for its two or three likely actions
  • Working surface or deep tool: Books, the Kitchen, the Studio, the world

Rules kept explicit

  • Planned is not purchased; scheduled is not paid; preparation is not a memory
  • The world always shows now; a past month lands at its bed with a banner
  • Weather, sun, terrain and the seven hosts never encode money (bills leave the sky: D18)
  • Opening, viewing, zooming and stepping in post nothing
Little thing: the Atlas nook’s island model becomes this island. The same low-poly Horizon on the kitchen table, your beds and your lanterns on it, small enough to pick up with your eyes.
Chapter 1¾

Time is a walk, and a plan is a card

The Journey used to tell time four ways at once: a spiral of months, stepping stones in the simple view, a month ring with a gate, and weather for the future. On the Horizon it tells time one way. And the Plan Studio, three studios and three rituals today, becomes one table.

The day is the unit

The Year Walk is a road of stones. Every day is a paving stone. A month is the stretch from the previous station to its own, 28 to 31 stones ending at a garden. Every seventh stone is a flagstone with a lantern post, lit when the week’s books are stamped. A year is one lap. An era is a gate across the road where it ended. You stand on today: the camp’s tent and lantern are on this stone.

Behind you the stones are worn and keep their day’s marks: a coin for a posting (its amount on tap), a leaf for a ritual held, a stamp for the week. Ahead, this month’s stones are laid and scheduled things stand on their stone in plain words: a bill is a paper slip on a post with its amount, a payday a pennant, a plan step a stake with the card’s name, the Sitdown two chairs, the Chapter close the station’s gate ajar. Beyond the station the next stretch is stakes and string until its month begins. The future is planned, not built. Walking back is replay; the slider is gone. The strip under the map is the same stretch drawn straight; drag it and the camp walks.

Underneath all of it is one read model, the day ledger: the stones, the strip, the Calendar grid, the Desk’s Leaving page and the camp card all read the same list of what a day holds. One truth per day.

The Plan Studio is the kitchen table

A plan is a recipe card: What, How much, By when, From which pot, Who. “One Pull Raises It” is the front door: five questions, one card, and the pull that shows the consequence. The card writes itself as you answer, so nothing is lost. A card lives on the wall and, at the same time, as a stake on the Walk at its “by when” stone, as slips on the stones where it pays or saves, and as a cat in the Loft if it saves. Change the card and all four change. Cards are “every month” (they roll forward until you take them down) or “once” (they live until their date, then join the past sheets). Turn a card over for its steps, which are the Glasshouse’s pots, and its evidence.

The drawer is the kitchen table’s objects: past sheets, tracing paper, the letter tray, two chairs, the recipe box, the kitty on the corner, the desk lamp, sticky notes. Everything the old twelve-section studio did is still there as a thing you touch; nobody has to open the drawer to make a plan. The three rituals become one: five beats at the Campfire, once a month, two people. Arrive. Look back. Settle. Look ahead. Seal. The seal closes the Chapter, edges the month’s bed and walks the camp on.

Chapter 3

Little Harbour, rebuilt on a slope

The harbour town keeps the v2 square, storefronts and pavilion, but moves onto a hillside so it has an upper street, a market stair and a quay. That gives the town its skate line, its views and its lantern row.

The square is where the default zoom-in from the Journey map lands (the map itself is home, Chapter 1½): the Queen, the mailbox, the Court's warm stone, the Fund bank on its north side and Our home on its east side with the window toward the dam. Behind the square the upper street runs along the slope with the gondola base at its end. Down from the square the market stair drops in three flights with a ramp beside it (step-free twin), through the lane of storefronts, to the quay.

The quay is Lantern Row made real: a line of lantern posts along the water that light at dusk, the ferry pier, the floatplane dock at the east end, the race finish with its run-out at the west end by the Quay Bridge. The Boathouse stands across the river mouth on the Landing side, so the town and the Boathouse look at each other over the water.

Materials: warm stone, terracotta, timber, porcelain accents, as the Classic dressing has them; clapboard and rope in Newfoundland; paper-tape and pressed flowers in Taylor.

Thresholds

The square and the market lane are pedestrian: boards park at the top of the stair and at the quay's west end. Wheels stop at the upper street's spur. The gondola boards from the upper street.

What the square sees

Looking north-west: the Reach, the High Span, the gorge, the dam's glass face with the Crown behind it. Looking south: the harbour, the Boathouse, the Lamp on the horizon.

Little thing: the harbour bell answers the summit bell. Ring one and the other replies a beat later, quieter, from across the island.
Chapter 4

The Crown and the Undercroft

The Mountain v2 massif, moved to the north-east and hollowed. Four doors lead inside: the Adit, the Throat, the Dam Gallery and the Sea Door. One railway runs through it. Nothing underground is an app building; it is the island's cellar.

Four doors

The AditA timbered mine mouth in the Hollow beside the Pottery Studio. The Ore Line's lower station. On foot or by cart. The ThroatA cave mouth high on the north face, wide enough to glide into. You come out of the sky and land on the Deep. Gliders only; the plane cannot fit and says so. Dam GalleryA stair inside the dam's east abutment, past the plaques, up into the Bell Gallery. The dam and the mountain are one structure. The Sea DoorA sea cave under the Prow. Row or take the ferry's small boat in; a jetty inside; steps up to the Deep.

Four rooms

Ore LineA mine-cart railway from the Adit through the mountain to the Crown station on the Shoulder: a way up the mountain underground, with one long drop and a splash through the Deep. Transport and ride in one. Lantern CaveGlow-worms on the ceiling. Every lantern you have found in the Hunt hangs here, lit. Empty on day one; full in a year. The DeepThe cavern lake under the summit, lit by the Throat's skylight. The underground river leaves it and comes out as the spring at the Reach. Canoe the Deep Run out of the mountain into daylight. Bell GalleryThe summit bell's rope comes down through the rock. Ring it from below and the whole island hears it.

How the Crown changes from v2

  • Moved off-centre to the north-east; the coast drive runs at its north foot.
  • The road, funicular and race are kept, re-routed; the funicular becomes the Ore Line's open-air top section.
  • The gondola now runs from the upper street in town straight up the south-east face to the Crown station.
  • The dam turns to face the town.

Underground rules

  • Dark but never unreadable: glow-worms, lantern light, the skylight.
  • Every door is a threshold with a clear mode change.
  • Calm view keeps it still and lit; reduced motion cuts the cart's drop to a fade.
  • No tool is moved underground. The Cellar stays in the house.
Little thing: the Throat's echo. Say nothing, land the glider on the Deep, and the splash echoes three times up the shaft.
Chapter 5

Seven neighbourhoods

Each has a porch, a threshold, a material, a sound and a flower. The sightline rule: from every porch you can see the next neighbourhood.

Chapter 5½

Room to grow: two reserves, eight plots

New ideas will come. Instead of expanding the island each time, two areas are graded, served and fenced from day one, with four plots each, sized to the largest building we have. They should carry a few updates before anyone has to move earth again.

The Terraces

Above Little Harbour on the Prow side, four stepped lots cut into the slope along Horizon Drive's first climb, with retaining walls from the v2 kit and sea views. The town grows uphill here. Served by the road (a lay-by per lot), the upper street's walk, and Town Weave passes below. Character: town, stone, terracotta. For ideas that want to be a building people visit.

Bight Shore

Four waterfront lots on the Bight's east shore between the Hollow and the Green, on a short spur off Green Road, with a jetty the rowboat and the Ferry's small boat can reach. Character: quiet, water, timber. For ideas that want to be a place rather than a building: a garden, a pavilion, a pier, a workshop.

Two small ones

A sealed drift in the Undercroft, beside the Lantern Cave, for an underground idea. An empty bay in the Flats' hangar for a second aircraft or vehicle.

What "in construction" looks like

  • A painted-card hoarding fence, surveyor's stakes with string, a neat timber stack, one sign per dressing: "Not yet" (Classic), a scrapbook "someday" tag (Taylor), a hand-lettered board (Newfoundland).
  • Meanwhile: wildflowers and a bench, both trivially removed.
  • No lanterns hidden in a plot, so the Hunt never breaks when one is built.

What is built now

  • The pad graded flat and the retaining walls placed.
  • Road spur or lay-by, path apron facing the door-to-be, a threshold marker.
  • A stable place ID reserved per plot in the WorldDefinition so nothing else is renumbered later.
  • A Sketchbook page never depends on a plot being empty.

Sizing

Each plot is 1.5 × the Library's footprint, the largest host we have, with 6 m clear on every side. Eight plots on 2000 × 1800 concept metres is under 2 % of the land; the Green's protected centre is untouched.

Chapter 9

The sky: designed before the ground

Launch heights the land provides, thermals where the rock is warm, landing fields that are already open, and gates that are things you would fly through anyway. A 300 m ceiling keeps the island the subject.

Launches

  • The Crown, 160 m: the long glide; you can reach the Lamp on a good day.
  • The Prow, 70 m: over the Stacks and back to Long Sands.
  • The Lamp gallery, 25 m: a short hop across the Bight mouth.
  • The plane, anywhere under the ceiling: bail out into the parachute.

Lift

  • Thermals over the Flats' ochre after noon and over the Prow's cliff face.
  • Ridge lift along the Crown's south face.
  • Sink over the Bight and the Notch (cold water, shade). Read the land to stay up.
  • The wind sim drives all of it; the windsock and the vane tell you.

Landings

  • The Green (target circle for the Drop Zone).
  • Long Sands (anywhere).
  • The Flats' strip (plane, glider).
  • The harbour and the Bight (floatplane).
  • The Deep, through the Throat (glider only).
  • A neighbourhood: fade to its nearest apron, no crash.

The gates (Ring Run)

The Needle's Eye arch → between the Stacks → under the High Span → through the dam's spillway arch → under the Bight Bridge → round the Lamp → into the Throat. Twelve gates in all; every one is a real structure with an underside.

The plane

One yellow bush plane, painted-card, with floats that swap for wheels at the hangar. A one-button "fly the ring" for anyone who just wants the tour; full stick for the Ring Run and Mail Day. It cannot enter the Throat and cannot land on a neighbourhood.

Rules of the air

  • No collisions with people, ever: you pass through and a gust says so.
  • The Look camera is the balloon; the guide map is what the balloon sees.
  • Reduced motion: flights become cuts between the twelve pages.
  • Nothing in the sky reads a balance.
Chapter 10

Pastimes

Fourteen things to do, none of which earns, unlocks or scores anything financial. Timed ones let Bianca's best run ride beside you as a paper ghost, household only.

Lantern Hunt

Sixty lanterns hidden across the island and under it, in the sea caves and on the stacks. Found ones stay lit at night, hang in the Lantern Cave, and mark the guide map. The island flyover, in Hearth's clothes.

Summit to Sea

The race, on S1, with branches onto S4 and the Notch shelf. Seventeen gates, the quay finish, "Open the Fund" at the end as now.

Ring Run

Glider or plane through the twelve gates in order. No clock unless you ask for one.

Drop Zone

Bail out over the Green; steer the parachute onto the target. The closer the ring, the bigger the puff of dandelion seed.

Mail Day

Fly the ring and drop paper parcels on seven rooftop targets. Purely play; nothing is delivered to a tool.

River Run & Deep Run

Canoe the Cup to the sea through the gorge's gates, or from the Deep out of the mountain into daylight at the Reach.

Nine Baskets

Disc golf across the Green and the Hollow. A walk with a purpose; the ninth basket is on the covered bridge.

Bocce

On the square's small green beside the Court. Two players, one jack, the Queen watching.

Ring Race

Bicycle lap of Horizon Drive against the ghost.

Ore Line ride

The mine cart's long drop and the splash through the Deep. Or the siding, for the quiet way through.

Chapter 10½

The sun: the island keeps real time

Jonathan’s caveat, made a design rule. The light follows the real sun in your time zone at 44° N, so the map was shaped around where the light falls: dawn on the harbour, noon on the Green, sunset on the airstrip, night on the lanterns.

The device clock and time zone are the source of truth; no network. Sunrise, sunset and the sun’s angle come from the date and a fixed latitude of 44° N, so June days are fifteen hours long and December days nine. The sun moves in 60-second steps; shadows re-render on the step, never per frame. Night is never black: a paper moon with the real phase gives the ambient a direction, and every door, threshold and edge reads at two in the morning in all three dressings.

Because the sun rises east and sets west, the island’s parts were placed to catch it. South-facing slopes hold the warm biomes (the Green, the orchard bowl); north-facing ones hold the cool (the woodland, the Throat, the snow that lasts). The dam faces south so it is lit all day and seen lit from the square. The square looks north-west, so at golden hour the dam and the Crown are lit while the square sits in warm shade: the first screen after sign-in is at its best in the evening, when people actually open the app. The Flats are west so the sunset lands on the strip and the balloon. The Prow is east so dawn flights start there.

Reduced motion and calm view freeze the sun at 15:30 in June light. Nothing waits for the sun: no pastime, view or tool is gated on the hour. The Lantern Hunt is better at night; it is not restricted to it.

Lights the island owns

  • Lantern posts on the quay, roads and walks, lit in sequence at civil dusk (Lantern Row, west to east, one per second)
  • The seven windows; the Glasshouse is the brightest thing at night
  • The Lamp’s beam every 8 s; the runway lamps; the observatory’s one warm dot
  • The Campfire; fireflies June to August
  • Glow-worms, found lanterns and the skylight underground
  • Running lights on the gondola, the Ferry and the plane
  • Aurora over the Crown, Newfoundland only, winter nights

Every page has a best hour

The square at golden hour; the Lamp gallery at sunset; the High Span in the morning; the Crown at noon; the Deep’s sun shaft at noon; the Flats at sunset; the quay at night. Acceptance captures are taken at the best hour and at its opposite, so both states are proven.

Little thing: the sundial in the square is real. Its gnomon’s shadow is the sun’s. Press and drag it to scrub the day for a look; let go and it snaps back to now. The harbour bell rings once at noon, softly.

The three dressings

Classic is the list above: an Ontario summer. Taylor keeps the species cut from patterned paper, with pressed cosmos and tulips at the edges and scrapbook tape on the milestones; concert-light bracelets only there. Newfoundland swaps clover for lupines, maples for black spruce, adds bakeapple and pitcher plants to the Reach, fog to the Notch, clapboard and root cellars to the town, and the aurora over the Crown on winter nights.

Little thing: the island keeps the date. The first trillium the week spring actually reaches Toronto; monarchs on the milkweed in August; the ice on Stillwater thick enough to skate in January; the Lamp lit at real sundown. Nothing financial touches any of it.
